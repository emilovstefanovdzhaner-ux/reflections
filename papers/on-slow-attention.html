On slow attention

14 Aug 2026 / 6 min read

For about two years I kept a spreadsheet of everything I read. Title, date started, date finished, pages, and a column I am still embarrassed about: pages per hour. I told myself it was curiosity. It was not. It was a way of turning something unmeasurable into something I could be good at.

The spreadsheet worked, in the sense that the number went up. I read more books in the second year than in the first. What I cannot tell you is what was in most of them.

The thing that speed removes

Reading quickly is not the opposite of reading well. Plenty of good reading is fast, and there is a kind of momentum that only appears when you stop stopping. What speed removes is not comprehension. It is the pause after comprehension — the two or three seconds in which a sentence you have just understood gets to argue with something you already believed.

Those seconds do not show up in any measure of progress. They look, from the outside and often from the inside, like doing nothing. I had trained myself out of them very efficiently.

I had confused finishing with understanding, because only one of the two has a date attached.

The clue was in my notes. Reading fast, I wrote summaries: accurate, tidy, and completely inert. Reading slowly, I wrote arguments — with the book, with myself, with someone I had spoken to a week earlier. The second kind is the only kind I have ever gone back to.

What I do now

I deleted the spreadsheet, which felt more dramatic than it was. Three habits replaced it, and only the third one really matters.

  • I read one book at a time, badly and out of order, and I let myself abandon it without ceremony.
  • I write in the margins, even when the margin note is just a question mark.
  • When something lands, I close the book and sit with it until the feeling either turns into a sentence or fades. Both outcomes are fine. The waiting is the practice.

That third habit costs almost nothing and is the hardest one to keep. There is always a small, insistent voice suggesting that I could be a page further along. It is the same voice that filled in the spreadsheet.

I am not making an argument against reading a lot. I am making a smaller one: that attention has a speed limit which has nothing to do with how fast you can move your eyes, and that most of what I have kept from books arrived in the moments when I was not, strictly speaking, reading at all.

The year without the spreadsheet was my worst year by volume. I remember all of it.
