The cost of certainty

2 May 2026 / 8 min read

In most rooms I have sat in, the person who spoke first was not the person who understood the most. They were the person with the highest tolerance for being wrong in public. For a long time I thought that was a flaw in the room. I now think it was mostly a flaw in me.

My habit was to wait. I would form a position, test it privately against the obvious objections, and only then say it out loud — by which point the conversation had moved on, or someone else had said a rougher version of the same thing and the group had already improved it.

What waiting actually buys

Waiting buys accuracy at the moment of speaking. That is a real thing and I do not want to pretend otherwise. What it costs is every correction that would have arrived if the half-formed version had been allowed out.

An idea kept private gets tested against one mind. The same idea, said badly to four people, gets tested against four, plus whatever those four have read, failed at, or been told by someone else. The private version feels safer because its errors stay invisible. They do not stop being errors.

Certainty is not a state you reach by thinking harder. It is usually a state you reach by narrowing what you are willing to consider.

The tell

There is a reliable signal I have learned to watch for in myself. When I want to be sure before speaking, I am almost never protecting the quality of the idea. I am protecting the impression that I have good ideas. Those two goals feel identical from the inside and pull in opposite directions.

The test I use is simple, if uncomfortable: would I still want more time if nobody could see me be wrong? Sometimes the answer is yes, and then the extra time is real work. Usually the answer is no, and then the extra time is just rehearsal.

Speaking earlier, without pretending

The fix is not confidence. It is labelling. Most of what I now say early comes with its own uncertainty attached, and that turns out to change how it is received.

  • “I think, and I have not checked this, that …” — invites the correction instead of daring it.
  • “Here is a bad version of the argument” — makes it safe for someone to hand back a better one.
  • “I have changed my mind since this morning” — said out loud, this costs far less than I spent years assuming.

None of that is humility exactly. It is closer to bookkeeping: saying how sure I am, at the same volume as what I am saying.

The papers on this site are an attempt at the same thing in slower form. Each one is a position I hold at the time of writing, published while I still might be wrong about it. That is the point. A reflection that waits for certainty is not a reflection. It is a summary of a conclusion I reached somewhere else, in private, where it could not be improved.
